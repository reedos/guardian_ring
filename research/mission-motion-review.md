## Original proposed experience

The following proposal records the original design reasoning. The implementation inventory above states what is now present; suggested simultaneous background highlights and optional civil-cadence controls below are not claims of implemented capability.

An opt-in **Watch mission** sequence can show a spacecraft moving around Earth while its Earth-facing payload observes and its communications equipment sends measurements to a receiving station. Treat the explanatory highlights as a pipeline: observation, readout and communications may overlap. The beats must not imply that the real spacecraft waits for an entire scene or full disk to finish before it begins transmitting.

| Explanatory beat | Visible action | Engineering meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observe | An Earth-facing aperture receives restrained incoming light glyphs. Keep collection visible during later beats. | Passive sensing receives scene radiation; the payload does not illuminate Earth with a scanning laser. |
| Read and prepare data | Highlight detector/readout, conversion and instrument interface in their established order. | Optical energy ends at the detector; analog signals, samples and formatted data use distinct paths. No onboard algorithm or throughput is assigned. |
| Downlink | Data glyphs move from an antenna to a ground receiver along a straight segment. | The visible glyphs identify direction and continuity, not radio speed, packet rate or link availability. |
| Receive and process | Separate receiver, processing equipment and operations/archival destinations become active. | Ground reception and product generation are distinct from onboard sampling. A generic control center is not a depicted operational facility. |

Power, thermal control and attitude holding continue as background functions, not sequential tasks that turn off between beats. A command uplink and returned engineering telemetry may be separate subdued paths; do not make image data return through the command arrow. Preserve manual part inspection: it pauses the mission, uses the safe rest pose and keeps the existing navigation contract. Reduced-motion users get a stationary, manually stepped mission.

## Original motion and quantity review

Use an explicit Earth-centered inertial view: Earth and ideal equatorial GEO spacecraft rotate together. In an Earth-fixed view the ideal GEO position is stationary. Do not spin Earth beneath stationary GEO markers and call that geostationary motion. The current shared `EarthSpin` hierarchy already gets this relationship right.

The current drawing uses compressed, nonuniform distance scales: GEO drawing radius is 2.5 Earth radii; the physical GEO reference radius is about 6.61 Earth radii. The authored HEO guide uses eccentricity 0.5, while the independent physics example uses 0.722. Its angular motion is correctly nonuniform, but its screen displacement is not a physical distance or velocity. Never derive a km/s display from those drawing coordinates or attach the model's HEO speed to the schematic marker as its instantaneous speed.

Existing engine outputs include period, altitude, perigee/apogee altitude, nadir distance and vacuum light time. Add **speed**, not an unlabeled scalar “velocity,” with `v = sqrt(mu * (2/r - 1/a))`. This is an ideal two-body, Earth-centered inertial speed. For a circle it reduces to `sqrt(mu/r)` and `2*pi*r/T`. For the current HEO model the output must say **at apogee**. Use the physical model's `a` and `r`, not `DRAWN_ORBITS`.

The following calculations use existing model assumptions, Earth GM 398600.435507 km³/s², equatorial radius 6378.137 km and mean sidereal day 86164.09054 s. They are **Calc.** rows, not spacecraft specifications:

| Independent example | Inputs already in model | Calculated speed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GEO reference circle | Altitude 35786 km | 3.075 km/s |
| MEO teaching circle | Assumed altitude 20000 km | 3.887 km/s |
| LEO teaching circle | Assumed altitude 1000 km | 7.350 km/s |
| HEO teaching ellipse, apogee | Half-sidereal-day period; assumed eccentricity 0.722 | 1.556 km/s |

The GEO reference altitude is rounded: its calculated period is about 0.10 s shorter than the adopted sidereal day. For exact geostationary motion, use the sidereal day as the animation period and explain that the altitude is a rounded reference, or derive the exact ideal radius from that period. Do not introduce artificial longitude drift to display this rounding difference.

The current playback rotates one sidereal day in 96.664 s, about 891.374 times real time. Label this as an **Assumed** display clock, or offer 1× and a plainly labeled accelerated clock. Orbit phase must be based on accumulated simulation time, so changing speed or pausing never changes position discontinuously. There should be no orbital thruster plume simply to indicate motion.

Radio/photon glyph timing needs a separate “motion slowed for explanation” note. Combining a many-times-faster orbit clock with seconds-long radio glyph travel does not create one physically scaled clock. Existing GEO nadir vacuum light time is approximately 0.1194 s, but that is not the time to the drawing's offset ground endpoint, processing delay or warning latency.
