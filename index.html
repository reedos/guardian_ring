The Guardian Ring

A planet in view. A signal in the dark.

Missile-warning satellites watch the whole Earth for the infrared heat of a rocket launch. Some sit in geostationary orbit, 35,786 km above Earth, keeping pace with the planet’s turn. Follow that heat from a rising plume, across space, through a telescope and a cold detector, and down to the ground.

01 / The ring

Stay above
the same Earth.

A geostationary satellite follows a circular orbit over the equator in step with Earth’s rotation. It stays above the same point on the ground, so it can watch the same part of the globe without a break.

One orbit is not enough. GAO’s public reports describe the Space Based Infrared System (SBIRS) with satellites in geostationary orbit and in highly elliptical orbits. The ring in the opening image is schematic: positions, spacing and satellite sizes are chosen to show the geometry.

What each orbit trades

Geostationary (GEO)

Persistence

Fixed over one longitude, the satellite sees the same wide disk of Earth all day. From above the equator, the far north and south appear only at a grazing angle.

See the geostationary view ↗
Highly elliptical (HEO)

The high latitudes

A stretched orbit is fastest near Earth and slowest at its far end, so the satellite lingers there. Tilt the ellipse, and that slow stretch can sit high above northern latitudes.

See the elliptical orbit ↗
Medium and low Earth orbit (MEO, LEO)

Closer, and more of them

Nearer orbits see a smaller patch of Earth and move across it, so covering the globe takes more satellites. In exchange, each one is closer to what it watches.

02 / The photon

Why watch
in the infrared.

A rocket’s exhaust is hot gas, and hot gases emit infrared radiation in characteristic molecular bands. Carbon dioxide and water vapor, two products of burning fuel, are the examples here, taken from a civil combustion study. Hot gas glows whether or not the Sun is up, so an infrared view works on the night side too.

CO₂ molecular emission bandNear 4.3 μm
H₂O molecular emission bandNear 2.7 μm

Wavelengths are in micrometers (μm), millionths of a meter. Visible light is shorter than one micrometer, so both bands sit in the infrared.

Light through air

Emission at the source and transmission through air are separate questions. Atmospheric gases absorb some wavelengths while transmitting others. What reaches orbit depends on both: what the hot gas emits, and what the air between lets through. This qualitative view supplies no transmission curve or real-system performance.

03 / The satellite

A sensor needs
a spacecraft.

The payload observes. The spacecraft supports it with power, communications, and paths for heat to leave. Military warning spacecraft are not documented in this detail, so this level borrows a published civil design: NOAA’s GOES-R weather satellite and its Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI).

Light

The infrared payload collects incoming radiation and directs it toward a detector.

Data

Electronics read the detector, process observations, and pass data through communications links.

Heat

Thermal paths move heat toward radiators. ABI provides a published example of a radiator releasing instrument energy to space.

04 / The payload

An instrument.
Designed as a whole.

The payload is the instrument the spacecraft carries. A telescope collects light and forms an image on a detector, electronics read the detector, and a cooler keeps it cold. Start with the assembled hardware, then open its enclosures to see the boards and connections inside.

An optical path

The drawing connects the telescope to the detector. In the named ABI instrument, the telescope forms a scene image at the focal-plane detectors.

Scanning or staring

An instrument can cover a scene in two broad ways. A scanning instrument sweeps its view across the scene with moving mirrors; ABI steers its line of sight with two scan mirrors. A staring instrument instead holds a two-dimensional detector array on the whole scene at once, with no sweep.

05 / The focal plane

Hold the image.
Manage the heat.

At the focal plane, light becomes an electrical signal. Cooling can reduce thermal noise, a benefit described in NIST's cryocooler review.

The cold region

Supports, electrical connections, and thermal paths meet at the detector assembly. ABI's published design moves heat from its focal planes through the cooler toward loop heat pipes and a radiator. The assembly above remains representative.

Why cold is hard

An infrared detector senses heat, so warmth inside the instrument competes with the light it is trying to measure. The detector has to run far colder than the spacecraft around it. A cooler pulls heat out of the detector, and that heat, plus the power the cooler uses, must still leave the spacecraft through radiators. Landsat 9’s civil TIRS-2 instrument gives a published example of how cold.

Civil example: TIRS-2 focal-plane design temperature43 K
Reading kelvinsThe kelvin scale counts up from absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature. One kelvin step is the same size as one degree Celsius. Glossary

Light arrives.
Charge is read.

From pixel to warning

A measurement
needs a path.

A reading on a detector is not yet a warning. It is processed on the spacecraft, sent to the ground, processed again, and passed to the people who act on it. Public sources describe the roles along that path, not how fast it runs.

  1. On the spacecraftRead the detectorReadout electronics turn each detector response into numbers, again and again
  2. On the spacecraftProcess on boardA mission processor handles the data before it leaves
  3. Through spaceSend it downRadio links carry the data to the ground at the speed of light
  4. On the groundProcess againA ground system operates the spacecraft and processes its mission data
  5. To peopleWarnProcessed results become a warning for the people who must act on it
What the public record says

Readout is the beginning of a data path. GAO's description of the Proliferated Warfighter Space Architecture names a spacecraft bus, infrared payload, mission processor, and communications equipment. These are public component roles.

In a separate architecture, GAO's description of the planned FORGE ground system identifies spacecraft operations and mission-data processing. These examples explain responsibilities across architectures; they do not specify a single connected system or a warning timeline.

Even the trip through space takes time: light needs about an eighth of a second to cross from geostationary orbit straight down to the ground, and processing and routing add more.
